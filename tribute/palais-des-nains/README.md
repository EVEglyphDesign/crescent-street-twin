# The Palace on Rachel Street — Le Palais des Nains

**961 rue Rachel Est · Le Plateau-Mont-Royal · 1926–1992**

> "We want people who are different to feel that they are part of us."
>
> « Nous voulons que les personnes différentes sentent qu'elles font partie de nous. »

From 1913 to 1992 a house on rue Rachel Est was open to the public because the couple who lived in it decided it would be. They built it to their own measure, set the hours, charged the admission and kept the proceeds.

---

## Philippe and Rose

Philippe Adélard Nicol was born on 27 September 1881 in Saint-Henri-de-Lévis, Québec, the only person in a large family born with dwarfism. His father stood 6 ft 3 in, his mother 5 ft 9 in. He never grew taller than about three feet.

He performed in vaudeville and with the Barnum & Bailey and Forepaugh & Sells Brothers circuses, and gave himself the stage title Count Philippe Nicol, in the tradition of General Tom Thumb.

In 1906, through Joseph-Octave Champagne — manager of the Québec strongman Louis Cyr — he met Rose Dufresne, born 17 June 1887 in Lowell, Massachusetts. She was also a little person: a pianist and a skilled cook who had made her own way through the circus circuit. They married in Lowell on 21 November 1906. The wedding drew such crowds that businesses and factories closed their doors for the ceremony.

Sources: [The Main — the history of Le Palais des Nains](https://www.themain.com/articles/palais-des-nains-montreal-history-rachel-street) · [ProposMontréal — l'histoire du Palais des Nains](https://proposmontreal.com/index.php/lhistoire-du-palais-des-nains/)

## The house at 961 Rachel

In 1913 they left circus life, moved to Montréal and opened their first attraction at 415 rue Rachel Est, now 507a.

In 1926, the year their son Philippe Jr. was born, they moved into a purpose-built house at 961 rue Rachel Est. From the street it was a standard Plateau triplex. Inside, every ceiling was lowered and every doorknob, mirror, stair, chair, lamp, picture frame, radio, piano and grandfather clock was built to their scale. Only the entrance hall kept a normal height. Two stone lions guarded the door.

It was open from 9:00 to 23:00, every day. Rose played piano in the parlour. Visitors of average height had to crouch to see themselves in the mirrors.

Sources: [Le Journal de Montréal — « Au Palais des Nains »](https://www.journaldemontreal.com/2017/01/28/au-palais-des-nains) · [Petite histoire du Plateau — Palais des Nains](https://blogue.histoireplateau.org/2006/07/04/palais-des-nains/)

## After

Philippe died on 26 May 1940. Rose died on 24 October 1964. After Rose's death, Huguette Rioux-Bastien, also a little person, ran a doll-repair workshop in the house and showed visitors the apartment from 1972 until it closed for good in 1992. The building has since been renovated beyond recognition; nothing of the interior remains.

Sources: [Material Culture Review — peer-reviewed study of the Midgets' Palace](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/MCR/article/download/18148/19526?inline=1) · [ProposMontréal — l'histoire du Palais des Nains](https://proposmontreal.com/index.php/lhistoire-du-palais-des-nains/)

## Why this is here

The couple controlled their own narrative. They set the terms, charged the admission, kept the proceeds, and built the room to fit themselves rather than shrinking to fit a room built for someone else.

That is the same principle this whole surface is built on: consent, and the right to set the terms of your own visibility.

Montréal was a city where that was possible — where difference could be met with curiosity and affection rather than pity or exclusion. We hold that up as the example.

## An honest note

We do not pretend otherwise. By today's standards, a paid tourist attraction built on watching a family live raises real questions. What we take from it is not the dime museum. It is the self-determination — and the fact that a city made room.

## What we could not verify

The following are deliberately absent because we could not confirm them from the five sources below. They are recorded here so that no one later mistakes silence for a claim.

- **The exact wording and attribution of the quoted line.** The sentiment "we want people who are different to feel that they are part of us" is presented as the spirit of the place, in both languages. We could not establish a verbatim, dated, first-person attribution of that sentence to Philippe or Rose Nicol from a primary source.
- **Admission price, and how it changed across the 1926–1992 span.** Referenced generally as "admission" without a figure.
- **Total number of visitors** over the life of the attraction.
- **Exact ceiling heights, door dimensions, or room count** of the interior. Sources describe the scaling qualitatively; we did not find measured figures.
- **Whether the two stone lions still exist**, and where they went after the 1992 closure.
- **Philippe Jr.'s life** beyond his 1926 birth year — his height, whether he lived in the house as an adult, and his dates.
- **The legal or ownership arrangement** under which Huguette Rioux-Bastien operated in the house from 1972.
- **The date and nature of the renovation** that erased the interior, and who carried it out.
- **The current condition or occupancy** of 961 rue Rachel Est.
- **Rose Dufresne's family background** and the circumstances that brought her onto the circus circuit.

Nothing in this document has been added from memory or inference. Where a source did not say it, it is not here.
